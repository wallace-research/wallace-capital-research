Business Overview

Mastercard Incorporated is a payment technology company connecting consumers, business owners, financial institutions, and governments through a secure network that facilitates electronic and online payments worldwide. Mastercard primarily functions as an intermediary that handles every step of the transaction progress, rather than operating as a sole card issuer. By doing so, the company benefits from the continuous expansion of global commerce without taking on the typical credit and other risks held by issuing banks. Mastercard’s primary revenue stream is transaction processing, or taking a small fee of every purchase made through their card systems, but the company also built upon these capabilities by expanding revenue generation opportunities such as assessment fees based on payment volume, cross-border transaction services, and a portfolio of value-added offerings. While core payment network operations remain the staple of the business, company management has additionally invested in services such as cybersecurity, fraud prevention, identity verification, data analytics, consulting, loyalty solutions, and open banking technologies, which all serve to strengthen both the consumer base and the fundamentals of Mastercard itself. By strengthening consumer relationships while reducing reliance on sheer payment volume alone, Mastercard positions itself as a broader financial platform intending to keep up with the rapidly shifting and increasingly unified network of payments globally. The nature of Mastercard is inherently asset-light; unlike traditional financial institutions, the company does not run any brick-and-mortar storefronts, nor do they finance loans or maintain a large balance sheet of consumer credit exposure. Instead, its primary assets consist of the payment network, technological infrastructure, trusted global relationships with thousands of financial institutions and millions of merchants worldwide, and the low debt the company carries allows for continuous reinvestment into expanding and buttressing the platform. As such, Mastercard can scale efficiently without incurring substantial increases in operating costs, supporting investors focused on companies with strong margins, robust free cash flow generation, and attractive returns. Mastercard’s decades of networking has created a self-nurturing effect, wherein every additional institution that issues Mastercard-branded payment products increases the value of the network to merchants, and broader merchant acceptance makes the network more attractive to consumers and issuing banks. Replicating or attempting to compete within an ecosystem of this scale would require enormous financial investment, extensive regulatory adoption, and taking on high risks, allowing Mastercard to maintain a strong competitive moat in the financial services industry. Wider trends in growth of e-commerce, international travel, commercial payments, and real-time payment solutions further expands Mastercard’s market, and the company appears to be addressing the potential setbacks that arise with global expansion through its reinvestments in ensuring consumer safety and platform stability. Overall, Mastercard has evolved far beyond a typical payment processor, and has grown into a diversified financial technology company backed by a strong reputation. Possessing multiple avenues for long-term growth, Mastercard combines its resilient business fundamentals, capital-light operations, and durable leadership to place itself in an envious position for sustained earnings growth and shareholder value creation.

Investment Thesis

  • Mastercard asserts itself as one of the highest quality and most durable businesses in the financial sector, and even the market itself. A key player on the S&P 500, usually within the top 20 most valuable companies by market cap, it is difficult to argue otherwise, especially when coupled with Mastercard’s compounding earnings year over year. The company possesses numerous competitive advantages, a capital-light business and operational model, and exposure to several long-term secular growth trends both within and outside of finance. Payments are shifting more and more away from cash, and whatever one’s opinion on that may be, it would be crass to not admit that Mastercard and its competitors will likely benefit well from this shift. Although the company often holds a premium valuation, and has experienced a consolidation period, trading in a range over the last year, its consistent earnings growth, disciplined capital allocation, and unparalleled competitive position and capabilities justify what might be a perfect time to invest for the long-term.
  • Arguably, Mastercard’s greatest advantage is its vast global payment network, which has been developed and constructed for almost 60 years at the time of writing since the company’s inception in December 1966, and further expand and grow. If electronic transactions continue to become prominent, as they’ve certainly demonstrated capability of, Mastercard’s growth potential will likely persist beyond past trends. Mastercard fosters existing and builds new mutual partnerships with financial institutions, business owners, and other merchants, with every new connection increasing the value of Mastercard’s network in a sustainable, organic way: both merchants and buyers gain access to the value of Mastercard. The wide reach of the company itself urges new consumers and financial institutions to adopt Mastercard-branded products. This reinforcing effect creates an ecosystem that would be arduous at best to replicate, and nearly impossible at worst. As such, Mastercard enjoys a rigorous economic moat within the financial services industry, arguing for long-term investors to help support this already resilient business.
  • Mastercard is far from only a personal credit card issuer. While Mastercard products include an expansive array of personal cards, from everyday standard cards designed for the average person’s purchasing power, to ultra-affluent cards offering elite global access and connectivity, to standard and enhanced debit cards, the company by no means is short of offerings for the individual consumer. However, Mastercard also leverages a plethora of business and commercial products such as business, corporate, and fleet cards, in addition to Mastercard Track: a secure system streamlining digital payments between buyers and sellers. As aforementioned, Mastercard is also expanding its offerings in the realm of services and technology solutions, notably ensuring the utmost security in payments both domestic and cross-border, as well as other cybersecurity and fraud protection features backed by AI and the latest frontrunning technologies. These services keep capital expenditures low and cement Mastercard as a consumer-focused company with a long horizon for continuous growth, simultaneously residing at or near the vanguard of financial products and services.
  • Mastercard also protects itself from many of the risks accrued by commercial banks. Mastercard generally does not issue loans, accept deposits, or assume consumer credit risk. Instead its fee-based transaction model allows revenue to expand alongside the consistent and growing payment volume of the Mastercard line of products, potentially providing a ballast of stable returns during economic downturns. Mastercard converts a large amount of its revenue into operating profit and free cash flow while requiring relatively little capital investment into its core business model. Thus, Mastercard has the rare potential of being able to comfortably experiment with new additions to the business, such as its successful cybersecurity efforts. Also, Mastercard’s operational prowess and managerial discipline ensure that the company will only invest in new ventures that are well-coordinated and have a high probability of success. The company has kept an organic investment mindset, promoting expenses that enhance the company’s technological capabilities and broaden its competitive position and economic moat, rather than pursuing aggressive expansion at the cost of profitability. Benefiting from considerable operating leverage, Mastercard allows profitability to expand even as incremental operating costs remain comparatively low. These characteristics often translate into industry-leading margins, strong ROI, and significant shareholder value creation, ultimately leading to a strong investment case for potential shareholders.
  • Mastercard has also shown a prioritization of its investors through share repurchases and steadily increasing dividends, positioning the stock as a quality grower. Ongoing investment in innovation and strategic acquisitions of lean startups amplify the outlook for investors by demonstrating a commitment to sustainable growth and maximizing shareholder value.
  • While Mastercard is not typically seen as a direct artificial intelligence investment, it can certainly be seen that the company is increasingly utilizing AI and machine learning throughout its operations to improve many areas of the business. AI also enhances the company’s data analytics capabilities and buttresses its growing portfolio of value-added services. As payment volumes increase, Mastercard may enable these technologies at a higher extent, potentially rooting them as a stable contractee of the AI era. Rather than relying on AI as its primary growth catalyst, Mastercard benefits by integrating these technologies into an already established and highly profitable business model, which strengthens operational efficiency and keeps risks far lower than AI-centered companies. Overall, Mastercard’s exceptional competitive moat, asset-light business model, reliable profitability, disciplined capital allocation, and exposure to historical and new growth trends all point towards the stock as a solid Buy. While risks to the company exist, and the share price itself has seen a bit of stagnation, the long-term landscape remains attractive. Mastercard, therefore, earns a Buy recommendation, especially for investors seeking a high-quality compounding business capable of delivering returns characteristic of quality growth companies over an extended investment horizon.

Valuation

The financial services industry is a notably resilient sector, and offers much-needed diversification for investors not wanting to overconcentrate in big tech runs. As such, Mastercard and its competitors operate incredibly similarly, and the prudent investor honestly would likely not go seriously wrong with any of the following listed competitors: Visa and American Express. PayPal also presents itself as somewhat of a competitor to Mastercard, offering a similar business model with more focus on online transactions than embedding into brick-and-mortar businesses and financial institutions. Thus, a picture of Mastercard’s current valuation can be gleaned by comparing these four renowned companies. As of Q2, Mastercard’s trailing P/E ratio is 29.72, sitting minutely below Visa’s trailing P/E of 29.91, and above American Express’s 21.11 and PayPal’s 13.00. Interestingly, and perhaps unsurprisingly, Mastercard and Visa operate incredibly similar business models that are almost synonymous with each other, thus demonstrating the mere decimal difference in trailing P/E. However, Mastercard’s trailing P/E being above American Express shows that investors are willing to pay more for Mastercard’s low overhead business model compared to American Express, the latter of which operates more of a standard card issuing model where the company assumes credit risk. For the forward P/E ratio, Mastercard’s is 26.18. Compared to Visa, American Express, and PayPal, each with forward P/E ratios of 22.88, 19.30, and 17.70 over the same timeframe, Mastercard possesses the highest of its near competitors. Mastercard’s elevated forward P/E ratio hints at investors hedging their bets that the company will remain highly profitable in the future, and are therefore willing to pay more of a premium compared to Visa. Mastercard’s PEG ratio is 1.58 as of Q2, demonstrating that the investor is paying a moderately high premium. These metrics all point to overvaluation. Certainly, Mastercard is not a cheap stock, but that does not mean that this should lead investors away immediately, unless buying moderately overvalued stocks is an antithesis of their investment philosophy. My DCF model illuminates that Mastercard is substantially overvalued despite its incredibly strong fundamentals, underscoring the rhetoric that it is a world-class business trading at a persistent premium. Despite this, history has repeatedly shown that investors purchasing exceptional businesses at reasonable, or even somewhat elevated, valuations frequently outperform those who hesitate indefinitely to wait for an ideal entry point that may never come to fruition. This especially is the case for dollar-cost-averaging investors, who intend to make periodic contributions over time, thus buying at highs and lows, creating a compounding effect that amplifies returns over the long run. Thus, Mastercard’s unique combination of durable competitive advantages, resilient cash generation, decades of profitability, lower risk (also seen in the beta), thoughtful expansions of value-added services, and role in long-term global spending trends suggests that the company’s quality growth characteristics may ultimately outweigh short-term valuation premiums. While periods of market volatility could certainly provide more attractive entry opportunities, which Mastercard has witnessed over the last year, the company actually seems to be in an opportunistic spot to buy, since it has not demonstrated a large uptrend at present. Overall, the current valuation does not appear excessive enough, and still in line with Visa, a prominent competitor, to warrant an undermining of the company’s compelling financials and long-term investment case. Although Mastercard is unlikely to represent a pure value opportunity at current prices, its premium valuation appears supported by the quality of the business. Investors should understand the expectations that near-term capital appreciation is unlikely to come from expansion, but those with a long-term horizon may view these prices as an acceptable entry point, thus reiterating the Buy recommendation.

Risks

Although Mastercard possesses an exceptional competitive position, vast economic moat, and compelling long-term fundamentals, the company carries considerable risks. In brief, Mastercard’s investment case heavily relies on the continued durability of its network, which in turn benefits from larger global trends in electronic payments. While these factors are not inherently a risk, nor do they invalidate the Buy recommendation per se, but they are important overarching trends to monitor occasionally when purchasing the stock, especially at an elevated valuation. First, Mastercard faces challenges from regulatory pressure. Regulatory scrutiny is almost inevitable for a company operating a global payment network that connects billions of cardholders and millions of merchants and financial institutions across myriad jurisdictions. As such, governments and regulators may seek to closely track Mastercard’s operational methodology, including payment processing fees, foreign transactions, and consumer protection. Mastercard’s scale and market position is pivotal to its success, but can also create hurdles for the company to manage. Of note, it is important to mention that if Mastercard faces strict regulatory action from a nation outside of the United States, then shareholder value may take a hit, which could puzzle some investors who only track the company’s American payment infrastructure and health. Domestically, Mastercard’s debit card program is being subjected to investigation at present by American government regulators as well, which could weaken the company’s grasp in a vital market. If overwhelming regulatory pressure becomes the norm, the company could face substantial economic challenges. Second, while Mastercard operates in a domain with a high barrier to entry, and thus does not face large-scale challenges from hundreds of competitors like some other sectors might, it is prudent to address Mastercard’s closest traditional transactional competitor, Visa. Visa operates almost entirely in parallel with Mastercard, and holds an extremely similar business model. Both companies operate worldwide, manage their own payment networks, and hold a strong balance sheet and overall profitability. As such, while competition from currently less prominent businesses such as American Express, PayPal, alongside digital wallet and fintech companies are not a major concern for Mastercard’s earnings and profit margins, Visa remains a fierce competitor, and if a smaller company gains a foothold, that could compound competitive intensity and light a hypothetical fire under Mastercard. Further, some governments are proposing to back their own payment systems, which could ultimately usurp many features that Mastercard offers, potentially presenting a particularly deleterious long-term challenge if such an incentive comes to fruition and achieves widespread adoption. Third, another crucial risk is cybersecurity. Mastercard depends intrinsically on trust from its many different consumers. A major cyberattack, prolonged technological disruption on the platform, data breach, or failure in fraud-prevention systems could easily damage the company’s reputation, leading current or potential customers away and resulting in heavy losses. An event of this caliber would also certainly heighten regulatory surveillance and increase operating expenses. While Mastercard has wisely been investing its capital into bolstering systems aimed at prevention of major cyber and fraud attacks, the risk still lingers. In many ways, the growth of AI presents both an opportunity and risk in this respect. AI can improve fraud detection and security, and Mastercard is already extensively promoting AI for this exact cause, it can also be leveraged by criminals to conduct more complex forms of cybercrime. Thus, Mastercard must continuously invest in technology to preserve high security standards and remain vigilant in fostering AI to protect protocols that underscore its competitive position. Fourth, while Mastercard avoids many macroeconomic conditions that traditional financial institutions must weather, the company still bears risks associated with consumer spending. Although the global nature of the business keeps this risk low, declining business activity, international travel, or a market recession could slow payment volume and transaction-related revenue. Mastercard’s asset-light business model certainly reduces the severity of some financial risks, but does not make the company entirely shielded from consumer-, commerce-, and economic-related risks. Fifth, valuation risk remains perhaps the most important concern for prospective shareholders. Mastercard’s premium valuation is a central aspect of the investment thesis, and implicit proof of the company’s long-term history of compounding returns. However, a premium valuation can also be a harbinger for disappointing returns. With the company trading at a higher P/E of several major competitors, substantiated by a DCF assessment demonstrating marked overvaluation, Mastercard will likely be suggested to sluggish returns if earnings growth slows, margins deteriorate, or any of the risks listed above manifest. Even if the underlying business remains strong and growing, the valuation multiple could limit returns for an extended period simply because traders are hesitant to continue buying in. While this does not limit the long-term upside potential necessarily, it is cogent to note that Mastercard is an unlikely candidate to produce an exceptionally high CAGR. While the company’s relatively low beta coupled with strong returns in the past certainly demonstrates Mastercard’s robust market position, it may not be the most appealing investment for investors seeking immediate growth. Ultimately, Mastercard’s risks are better characterized as tentative setbacks to the timing of shareholders returns rather than an immediate threat to the core of the business. Mastercard remains an incredibly durable and profitable company, and is likely a high-quality compounder for investors in the coming years. However, even exceptional businesses can generate mediocre returns, which prospective shareholders may wish to be vigilant of.

Conclusion

Ultimately, Mastercard presents a compelling opportunity for investors desiring a high-quality business capable of compounding shareholder value over the long-term. Its fundamentals are unique and well fortified, and possesses a few differing opportunities to organically promote growth. Some of these opportunities involve using direct investments from the company itself, while others play off of broader economic shifts in the modern era. The company’s financial performance further supports investors and the Buy thesis, with consistent revenue and earnings growth as well as regular share repurchases and dividend increases, demonstrating savvy capital allocation to spur both growth initiatives and investor sentiment. While Mastercard is not without its weaknesses, risks to the company do not outweigh its core strength. Mastercard has demonstrated an ability to adapt in parallel with changes in the ecosystem of the payments industry, and its wide economic moat creates little room for disappointment amongst investors seeking quality growth. Considerably, Mastercard is much more than a credit card company, rather it is a vital piece embedded into the global economy. As such, Mastercard earns a Buy recommendation. While the stock is not likely to be a pure value play, nor is it a sheer growth-heavy stock, Mastercard can still capture the unique blend of being an exceptional business with relatively low volatility, while also yielding impressive returns year over year. For long-term investors willing to pay a premium for an exceptional business, Mastercard’s competitive advantages stand out as a solid choice. If investors subscribe to the thesis that payment efficiency and global interconnectivity will expand, Mastercard stands out as incredibly lustrous.
